Wren moment

Wren found a cave. Here, the cave was an iron drill.

When money gets cheap to make, prices climb and whoever holds it pays. The makers did nothing wrong: they took up a better tool. The trouble is what happens to any money that suddenly gets easy to make. Ulm Hollow learned that with pentaja.

Sources
  • R. Williams, A Key into the Language of America (1643), ch. XXIV, “Of their Coyne”: Project Gutenberg. Stone drilling before European awls; whelk and quahog beads.
  • L. Jordan, Colonial Currency, University of Notre Dame: “Wampum: Introduction”, “Wampum in New Netherland”, “The Beaver Pelt in New Netherland” and “Charts Illustrating Wampum Value Depreciation” (coins.nd.edu, read via the Internet Archive). The 1637 law (6 beads a penny, sums under 12 pence); the beaver pelt at about 640 beads (1641) and 3,200 (1662); in silver the pelt stayed at 6–8 guilders; the 1661 repeal, nine years after the Boston mint opened.
  • Petition of the burgomasters and schepens of New Amsterdam, 19 Sept 1658, New York State Archives A1809 vol. 8 (translation via Mapping Early New York): wampum from New England, “so oversupplied that it is not considered valuable anymore”; the rate from 6 to 8 beads a stiver.
  • E. Peña, “Wampum Production in New Netherland and Colonial New York”, BEADS 2 (1990); and Peña (2006), via Jordan: metal tools let makers “work more efficiently”.
  • A. Woodward, Wampum (1878): Project Gutenberg. Long Island as the biggest producer; floods of “rough” beads and counterfeits; the 1661 repeal.
  • E. James-Perry (Aquinnah Wampanoag), “About Wampum”; McCord Stewart Museum, Wampum: Beads of Diplomacy. Belts are still made and used today.
