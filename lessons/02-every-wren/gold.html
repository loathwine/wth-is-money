Wren moment

Every money had a Wren.
Gold came closest to having none.

From 1849, the whole world dug for twelve years. Prices rose by up to a third, then fell back.

In real life: while Britain was on gold, prices swung by up to a third, yet were about the same in 1913 as in 1821. Like €100 still buying the same shopping 92 years later.

Yap, again: in 1932 France swapped its dollars for gold in New York. To save shipping it, bank staff just marked the drawers as French, and the papers wrote of a “loss of gold”. Money, like Yap's stones, doesn't have to move to change hands. (Friedman, The Island of Stone Money, 1991)

Sources
  • British prices (the jar in the gold round, the result screen and “In real life”): Bank of England, A millennium of macroeconomic data for the UK v3.1, sheet A1, consumer price index: 1849 1.112, 1851 1.043, 1856 1.352, 1860 1.292, 1867 1.405, 1873 1.381, 1890s average 1.091, 1821 1.257, 1913 1.244. Loaves = 8 × CPI(1849) ÷ CPI(year): the loaf is our unit, the ratios are real. Britain was on gold from 1821 to 1914.
  • World gold mined a year: Encyclopaedia Britannica (1911), “Gold”, Table I (Soetbeer's series): about 57 t a year in 1841–50 and about 198 t a year in 1851–60.
  • How much the rush added: W. S. Jevons, Investigations in Currency and Finance (1884), reprinting A Serious Fall in the Value of Gold Ascertained (1863): a lasting fall in gold's value of “at least” 9%, in his view “nearer 15 per cent”; Newmarch's estimate that the new gold of 1849–60 was “about 50 per cent” of the gold then in use.
  • Prices on gold in the long run: M. D. Bordo, “Gold Standard”, Concise Encyclopedia of Economics: low inflation on average, but “highly unstable in the short run”.
  • All the gold ever mined, about 220,000 t (a cube of about 23 m): World Gold Council, How much gold has been mined?
  • The 1932 earmarking: M. Friedman, The Island of Stone Money, Hoover Institution WP E-91-3 (1991). Much of that gold was shipped to France later in 1932; the note claims only the earmarking.
  • The Wrens of history: each chapter's own sources (Yap, beads, wampum, cowries, Rome). Cowries: about 2,000 shells to a silver dollar in the late 1840s, 20,000 in the 1880s (Dahomey). Rome: wheat in Egypt, 8 → 300 drachmas a sack, AD 100 → 293.
  • Game values: the world's pile in 1849 (6,000 t, so the rush adds ×1.36; Newmarch's estimate would give about ×1.5), the 1849/1850 split of the first rush years, the jars in rounds 1–5 (each halves when its pile doubles; Yap's 8 → 7 follows the later accounts that O'Keefe's stones were priced lower, the 7 is ours), the pour speeds, the ×2.6 cut-off and the points.
